# Predictive subspaces track the clock, not the prior

Yes, there is a credible path to publication, but not by writing up the current secondary-motor-cortex result on its own. The most defensible paper is a methods paper (Route 2). It would show, with theory, simulation and brain-wide International Brain Laboratory (IBL) data, that long-horizon predictive and slowness objectives are captured by session-scale nonstationarity, and it would supply a valid control battery. Its scientific core would be a decomposition of the slow mode into probe motion, unit turnover and brain state (the drift-versus-state part of Route 1). The block-prior geometry result (Route 3) would serve as a worked example of what these subspaces miss. Across five review questions and roughly 1,100 screened records, no published paper or preprint was found that does any of the following: applies long-horizon predictive subspace methods to neural data and reports that the leading component tracks session time; adapts pseudo-session tests (Harris's null built from the task's own random generative process) to unsupervised subspaces; uses IBL's public per-insertion probe-motion traces to test whether a slow population mode is artifact or state; or measures where the block prior sits relative to dominant population variance. Each of these gaps is real, but each has close neighbours. The biggest scoop risk is in-house IBL work visible on GitHub. An IBL staff scientist has computed per-trial engagement probabilities for every Brain-Wide Map trial, and since **17 October 2026** has been running a brain-wide stillness, pupil and arousal pilot with explicit slow-drift controls ([GitHub engage](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/engage); [GitHub default_mode_network](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/default_mode_network)). That makes the pupil, engagement and arousal half of Route 1 the riskiest piece to build alone. The main threat to Route 2 is not being scooped but reviewers calling the result obvious, because classical time-series theory already predicts that the most predictable components of a drifting series are its nonstationary ones. Route 3's main threat is that the result is expected unless it is backed by a predictability test that ignores variance. These conclusions rest on targeted searches without Google Scholar cited-by crawls, and many details were checked only at abstract level, so the report ends with a short list of checks to run before committing.

## The project's three findings sit on contested ground

The project analysed Neuropixels recordings from the IBL Brain-Wide Map (BWM). Neuropixels probes are high-density silicon electrodes that record hundreds of neurons at once. The BWM is a standardised, consortium-wide dataset of mice performing the same visual decision task. The project used 16 sessions from 13 mice, restricted to secondary motor cortex (MOs, also called M2). It fit lagged reduced-rank ridge regression (RRR): a linear map, constrained to low rank and penalised by a ridge term, that predicts the population's activity a fixed lag into the future from its activity now. With $x_t$ the vector of binned unit activity at time $t$, lag $\Delta$, rank $d$ and ridge penalty $\lambda$, the fitted map is

$$\hat{B}_\Delta = \arg\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le d}\ \sum_t \left\lVert x_{t+\Delta} - B\,x_t \right\rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert B \rVert_F^2 .$$

The column space of the fitted map is the "predictive subspace" at that lag, also called the horizon. The project compared these subspaces at lags of 0.5, 2, 8 and 30 seconds with the "top variance subspace", meaning the leading principal components from principal component analysis (PCA).

The task variable of interest was the block prior. In the IBL task a visual grating appears on the left or right. The probability that it appears on the left alternates between 0.2 and 0.8 in uncued blocks of variable length. The Bayes-optimal block prior is an ideal observer's running estimate of which block is in force, given the stimuli seen so far ([UNIGE abstract of Findling et al.](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:188000)).

The project reached three findings. First, the prior can be decoded from the full MOs population but not from the top variance or predictive subspaces. Second, the long-horizon predictable structure is roughly one-dimensional and shared across horizons, tracks elapsed time in session with $R^2 \approx 0.84$, and does not keep the same direction when the session is split into halves. Third, units drift in and out of the recording in 14 of 16 sessions.

The review shows that each finding touches an active debate. A one-dimensional slow mode resembles the "slow drift" that macaque labs treat as a genuine arousal or impulsivity state. Units appearing and disappearing are the signature that spike-sorting papers attribute to probe motion. Spike sorting is the step that assigns recorded voltage spikes to putative single neurons, called units. The absence of the prior from dominant subspaces is what the mouse movement-and-arousal literature would predict. The three candidate routes take these debates in turn. Route 1 asks whether the slow mode is a brain state (arousal, engagement, satiety) or a recording artifact (probe drift or spike-sorting instability), across the whole brain. Route 2 asks whether long-horizon predictive and slowness methods in general recover nonstationarity rather than task variables, and how to control for it. Route 3 asks where the block prior lives in population activity across brain regions, if not in high-variance or predictable directions.

## Q1: Slow drift is an established one-dimensional state in monkeys but unvalidated brain-wide in mice

The anchor paper is Cowley and colleagues' 2020 Neuron study of macaque V4 and prefrontal cortex. They averaged stimulus-subtracted spike counts in 20-minute windows stepped by 6 minutes and ran PCA on those slow averages. The first component, the "slow drift axis", explained about **70% of slow variance** (69% and 77% in the two monkeys) and evolved over timescales of about 33 and 44 minutes ([Cowley et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822647)). The drift was nearly identical in V4 and prefrontal cortex recorded on separate arrays, with median absolute correlations of 0.96 and 0.93 against 0.68 for a random-walk null. It co-varied with hit rate, false-alarm rate, pupil size and reaction time, and removing it improved decoding of false alarms. The authors therefore read it as an impulsivity-like state rather than a change in sensory encoding (same source). Follow-ups tied the same axis to pupil size and microsaccade rate ([Johnston et al. 2022, Cerebral Cortex](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhab418)) and to scalp EEG alpha power, which correlated with the drift at a median of minus 0.34 ([Johnston et al. 2022, eNeuro](https://www.eneuro.org/content/9/3/ENEURO.0012-22.2022)). The most recent extension found the drift in superior colliculus. There it shared only a median 0.26 of its variance with prefrontal drift, and pupil explained about 6% of it, which shows that even a genuine slow state can have modest external correlates ([Johnston & Smith, eLife, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13246003/)).

Two details of this literature matter directly for the project. First, Cowley et al. kept only units whose waveforms stayed stable across the session. They showed that the drift persisted without visual stimuli and during still fixation, and they aligned the axis sign across sessions using stimulus responses. Their claim of a genuine state therefore rests on artifact controls plus agreement across simultaneously recorded areas plus behavioural covariation, not on a time trend. Second, no paper in this literature reports a split-half test of whether the axis keeps its direction within a session. With sessions of two to three hours and 20-minute windows, each session yields only a handful of independent slow samples. The project's failure to reproduce the direction across halves is therefore ambiguous. It could mean there is no stable state, or it could mean too few effective degrees of freedom to pin down a direction.

In mice, the closest anchor is Okun and colleagues' 2019 study of medial prefrontal cortex with Neuropixels. Below 0.1 Hz (the "infraslow" range), the population rate was strongly coherent with pupil area. **65% of neurons (350 of 541)** were significantly coherent with pupil at 0.03 Hz, with two groups in opposite phase: some cells fired more with arousal and others less ([Okun et al. 2019, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458908)). That mixed-sign structure is a fingerprint a uniform recording artifact would struggle to imitate. At the brain-wide scale, two 2019 papers showed that within-session motivational change reorganises activity. Allen and colleagues recorded about 24,000 neurons in 34 regions as thirsty mice drank to satiety. They found that brain-wide activity tracked satiety and that stimulating thirst-sensing neurons restored the "thirsty" population state, which is causal evidence for a genuine state ([Allen et al. 2019, Luo lab PDF](https://luolab.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj20556/files/media/file/allen_et_al_science_2019_0.pdf)). Steinmetz and colleagues found that pre-stimulus activity across 42 regions predicted task engagement ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337579254_Distributed_coding_of_choice_action_and_engagement_across_the_mouse_brain)). Analyses of the eight-probe Stringer and Steinmetz recordings found that ensembles were local to a region below about one second and spanned multiple regions above it ([Network Neuroscience 2023, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10312261/)). That motivates comparing slow modes across simultaneously recorded probes.

A parallel debate over "representational drift" (gradual change in how neurons respond to the same stimulus) supplies the template for controls. Sadeh and Clopath argued that running and pupil-linked behaviour explain much apparent drift in Allen Institute data. Their eLife reviewers pressed them to separate behaviour from elapsed time, because the two are collinear within a session ([eLife peer reviews](https://elifesciences.org/articles/77907/peer-reviews)). Deitch, Rubin and Ziv analysed the same data and concluded that behavioural state does not drive drift ([Current Biology](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(21)01052-6)). That exact confound, time versus state, is the one the project's slow mode faces.

## Q2: Nobody has separated probe drift from brain state in a slow population mode

The size of the artifact problem is now well documented. Allen Institute documentation for its acute head-fixed Neuropixels recordings states that the probe drifts "on average, about 40 microns" over roughly three hours. It warns that unit quality metrics assume stable waveforms and recommends plotting amplitude over time to catch units "drifting below threshold" ([AllenSDK docs](https://allensdk.readthedocs.io/en/latest/visual_coding_neuropixels.html)). Kilosort4 surveyed more than 500 IBL recordings and found only **11 with large continuous drift spanning at least 40 µm**, which is the spatial repetition period of a Neuropixels probe. It also built its simulated drift scenarios from patterns seen in IBL data ([Kilosort4, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732)). So gross drift is rare in IBL, but drift of tens of microns is routine. Tens of microns is comparable to a unit's spatial footprint: UnitMatch reports that waveform amplitude decays by 10% over 30 to 95 µm ([UnitMatch, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11978519/)). Modest residual motion can therefore push low-amplitude units below detection threshold or cause them to be split and merged. That produces exactly the pattern of units entering and leaving that the project saw in 14 of 16 sessions. The IBL's own reproducibility paper says so directly: "severe discontinuity will cause units to artifactually disappear/reappear" ([IBL reproducibility paper, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12068871)).

The motion-correction tools reveal a two-way confound that matters for any test of state against drift. Motion estimators infer probe movement from how spike amplitudes and depths are distributed over time, and they assume that distribution is roughly stationary. The SpikeInterface motion benchmark deliberately included firing rates that rise and fall sinusoidally with a three-minute period. It found that every method "has some intrinsic limitations" and that the best method depended on the scenario ([Garcia et al. 2024, eNeuro, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10897502/)). MEDiCINe simulated neurons appearing and disappearing with ramping rates, and it notes that "neuron firing rates may fluctuate over time, neurons may be gained or lost" ([Watters et al. 2025, eNeuro, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11896784/)). A strong, depth-structured, genuine change in firing rate could therefore leak into the motion estimate. The IBL drift trace is not a perfectly independent nuisance regressor, and a test that uses it needs a second, more independent check. The candidates are motion estimated from the low-frequency LFP band (DREDge offers this; [SpikeInterface how-to](https://spikeinterface.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/how_to/drift_with_lfp.rst.txt)) or comparisons across probes.

On downstream consequences, the literature is lopsided. Turnover across sessions is well studied. Gallego and colleagues showed that latent dynamics stay stable across up to two years despite steady neuron turnover, once each session's latents are aligned ([Gallego et al. 2020 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/447441v3.full.pdf)). UnitMatch found that 29.5% of the units Kilosort called identical across days were not matched by fingerprint ([UnitMatch, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11978519/)). Sorting errors are known to bias pairwise correlations ([Ventura & Gerkin 2012, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22529350/)). A detailed cortical simulation reports that sorting distorts manifold geometry (this comes from a secondary summary; [Laquitaine et al., bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805v2.full)). Trautmann and colleagues showed that merging units on the same channel barely distorts population state ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/229252v2)). But Q2 found **no study that quantifies how within-session residual drift or unit dropout biases slow latent factors, principal components or dimensionality estimates**. The mechanism is simple to state. If a subset of units changes its presence or gain monotonically with session time, those changes are shared in time. PCA, factor analysis and Gaussian-process factor analysis will all collect them into one high-variance slow factor that looks like an elapsed-time mode.

The decisive infrastructure finding is that **the test the project needs is feasible with public data**. Querying OpenAlyx returned **1,527 probe-insertion collections** with the three drift datasets (`drift.um`, `drift.times` and `drift_depths.um`). One parsed example had about 2.19-second time bins and nine depth blocks 400 µm apart, with displacements from about minus 44 to plus 46 µm ([OpenAlyx datasets endpoint](https://openalyx.internationalbrainlab.org/datasets?name=drift.um.npy); access instructions in the [ONE public data notebook](https://int-brain-lab.github.io/ONE/notebooks/public_one.html)). These are the non-rigid motion estimates (estimated separately at each depth block) from IBL's Python port of Kilosort 2.5, the ibl-sorter ([GitHub ibl-sorter](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/ibl-sorter)). The IBL software also contains a simpler estimator, `estimate_drift`, that recomputes a single rigid drift trace from released spike times, amplitudes and depths ([IBL brainbox source](https://docs.internationalbrainlab.org/_modules/brainbox/metrics/electrode_drift.html)). Two caveats apply. These traces record the motion that was corrected, not residual uncorrected motion. And it is unverified whether the 16 MOs sessions use the original or the `2024-05-06` re-sort, or whether the released spike depths are motion-registered.

From these pieces Q2 assembled a five-part separation strategy that nobody appears to have combined. The first part regresses each unit's activity on the motion trace at its own depth: an artifact predicts that units change with local motion. The second tracks per-unit amplitude and depth over time: artifact-driven dropout is preceded by amplitude falling toward threshold, while a genuine rate change leaves amplitude intact. The third compares slow modes across two simultaneously recorded probes: a global state should be shared, often with mixed-sign loadings, while probe-local artifacts should follow each probe's own motion. The fourth regresses the mode on pupil, wheel, lick and engagement covariates. The fifth replicates the mode in sorting-free signals such as multi-unit threshold crossings per depth bin, which bypass unit identity. Two further signatures come from adjacent work. Brain-state transitions mainly change the spontaneous rate of slow-firing neurons ([Cell Reports 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S221112472301197X), secondary). And brain-wide arousal recruits opposing neuronal populations ([Nature 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10350-9), secondary). Both predict mixed signs and firing-rate dependence for a state, against amplitude dependence for an artifact.

## Q3: IBL nulled slow drift out of its flagships, and its staff are now circling state

Neither IBL flagship treats the slow mode as an object of study. The BWM paper covers **139 mice, 12 labs, 699 insertions and 75,708 well-isolated units** ([BWM paper, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408349/)). It names slow probe drift as a source of spurious correlations across trials. It controls for drift with pseudo-sessions, synthetic sessions and "imposter" sessions, and with within-block permutations for block-related tests. It shows that performance falls and movement times lengthen toward the end of sessions but never analyses this neurally (same source). The peer-review file adds two important facts. First, the objective block-prior analysis was **removed from the BWM paper during review** and moved to the companion prior paper. Second, when a referee asked about within-session variability, the authors replied that "within-session differences are dominated by factors such as satiation/fatigue that pose interpretational and technical difficulties (for an instance of the latter, the effect of Neuropixels drift)". As a "simple test" they correlated a sliding 20-trial reward rate with overall inter-trial firing rate and found "no systematic correlation" ([BWM peer-review file](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-025-09235-0/MediaObjects/41586_2025_9235_MOESM3_ESM.pdf)). This exchange is useful for framing. IBL looked at the question coarsely, found a null at the level of overall firing rate, called the problem hard and left it open.

The companion prior paper by Findling, Hubert and colleagues decodes the Bayes-optimal prior from activity in the inter-trial interval, 600 to 100 ms before stimulus onset. In the Nature version, the prior was significant in **73 of 242 regions (30.2%)** in electrophysiology and 58 (24.0%) after merging with widefield imaging ([Findling et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408363)). The preprint reports somewhat different counts (47 of 159 regions, 29.5%), so a manuscript should cite the published numbers ([bioRxiv v3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.04.547684v3.full)). The authors attribute spurious prior decoding to "drift in the Neuropixels probes" and use 1,000 pseudo-sessions per session. They exclude trials only by reaction time and sessions only by trial count. **No control for time in session or engagement is described, pupil-linked arousal was not analysed as a confound, and there is no subspace or variance-share analysis** ([Findling et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408363)). The IBL reproducibility paper likewise treats drift only as a quality-control gate ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12068871)).

Other work on IBL data falls into neighbouring but distinct niches. A family of behaviour-only models built on the GLM-HMM (a hidden Markov model whose states are each a separate regression of choice on stimulus and history) finds that mice switch between engaged and disengaged or biased strategies within sessions ([Ashwood et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-021-01007-z); [Cuturela et al. preprint](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.1101%2F2024.11.30.626182)). One of these reports that greater past reward predicts transitions to disengagement, "suggesting that disengagement may be associated with satiety" ([Mohammadi et al. 2025, Nature Communications](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.1038%2Fs41467-025-66738-0)). Yin and Hiratani relate a cortical timescale measured in inter-trial and passive periods to each animal's disengagement tendency. Their analysis works between animals, not within sessions, and it does not address drift ([bioRxiv record](https://api.biorxiv.org/details/biorxiv/10.1101/2025.07.11.664420)). Shi and colleagues map intrinsic timescales of 27,960 neurons across 223 areas using the end-of-session passive period, with no detrending or drift discussion ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12407892/)). Zhang and colleagues built a multi-session reduced-rank regression decoder across 433 sessions and 270 regions, targeting choice, prior, wheel, whisking and pupil. It is a decoder from neurons to behaviour over within-trial windows, not a model from neurons to future neurons, and it has no slow-mode analysis ([Neuron 2025, Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.1016%2Fj.neuron.2025.10.026)). A Cosyne 2026 poster added a model of "slowly varying fluctuations" in background rate to the PPSeq sequence detector. On IBL motor cortex it raised population-activity $R^2$ from about minus 1.6 to 0.62, but it treats the slow component as a nuisance ([Cosyne 2026 program book](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6102ca347474c263c40150cd/t/69b2ee5acab23a50003b7f26/1773334106922/Cosyne2026_program_book.pdf)). Outside IBL data, a June 2026 arXiv paper shows that geometric reliability (representational similarity) falls with within-session lag in the Steinmetz 2019 data and that interleaved train and test splits inflate reliability ([Raju, arXiv 2606.29655](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29655)).

The scoop-risk evidence is on GitHub. The `engage` repository (created June 2025, sole contributor Michael Schartner) adapts Ashwood's GLM-HMM to every BWM mouse. It ships a parquet file with a **per-trial engagement probability for all BWM animals** ([GitHub engage](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/engage)). The `default_mode_network` repository, created **17 October 2026** and last pushed **5 November 2026**, describes "pilot analyses" over 419 sessions, 137 mice and 54,934 neurons. It includes "brain-wide stillness correlates" and an arousal report applying the Landemard et al. 2026 Arousal+ and Arousal− model. Its stillness sub-project reports lower firing during awake stillness in 129 of 130 mice and includes a script using "shared 10 s / 30 s windows to limit slow-drift confounding" ([GitHub default_mode_network](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/default_mode_network)). A third repository holds the Schartner, Liu and Fiete BWM manuscript. It works on trial-averaged responses and includes an RRR fit, with nothing on session-scale modes ([GitHub bwm_dmn](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/bwm_dmn)). ## Q4: Predictive objectives are drift detectors by construction, yet no neural paper says so

Every method that ranks components by how predictable or slow they are assumes stationarity, explicitly or implicitly. The clearest statement comes from Dynamical Components Analysis (DCA; Clark, Livezey and Bouchard, NeurIPS 2019). DCA finds the linear projection whose past and future windows of length $T$ share the most mutual information under a Gaussian model. Writing $\Sigma_T$ for the covariance of $T$ consecutive projected samples, it maximises

$$I_T(V) = \log\det \Sigma_T\!\left(V^\top X\right) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log\det \Sigma_{2T}\!\left(V^\top X\right).$$

The authors write that "stationarity is a fundamental conceptual assumption of our method" and list non-stationary extensions as future work. They also mean-centred their motor-cortex data with a **30-second sliding window**, which quietly removes components slower than about 30 seconds before DCA sees them, and used prediction windows of roughly a second ([DCA full text, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.09944)). A trend or within-session random walk keeps its autocovariance close to its variance across many lags, so it makes the second term small relative to the first and scores highly. A long-horizon application to IBL data without that preprocessing falls outside DCA's own stated assumptions.

The same logic has deep roots outside neuroscience. Box and Tiao's 1977 canonical analysis orders linear combinations of a multivariate time series from least to most predictable. In the cointegration literature, the most predictable directions of a nonstationary series are its unit-root directions, the non-reverting components that wander like random walks ([Velu et al. 1987 note](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jtsera/v8y1987i4p479-487.html); [Galeano & Peña review](https://halweb.uc3m.es/esp/personal/personas/dpena/publications/ingles/2000RESENHAS_galeano.pdf)). In machine learning, time-contrastive learning (the ancestor of CEBRA's time-based mode) recovers sources provably *because* they are nonstationary ([Hyvärinen & Morioka 2016, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.06336)). Lagged RRR fits this pattern. Consider a latent mode whose autocorrelation decays exponentially with time constant $\tau$. The best linear prediction of the mode from its own value $\Delta$ seconds earlier explains a fraction of its variance equal to the squared lag-$\Delta$ autocorrelation:

$$\rho(\Delta)^2 = e^{-2\Delta/\tau}.$$

At a 30-second horizon, a mode with a 5-second time constant keeps a predictable fraction of $e^{-12} \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}$. A mode with a 30-minute time constant keeps $e^{-1/30} \approx 0.97$. Only session-scale processes survive at 30 seconds, whether they are drift, arousal, engagement or satiety. That is why the project's long-horizon subspace collapsed onto one dimension that is shared across horizons.

Neuroscience has published the variance-based version of this warning but not the predictive one. Antognini and Sohl-Dickstein showed that most of the variance of a high-dimensional random walk falls in its first few principal components ([NeurIPS 2018](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/hash/7a576629fef88f3e636afd33b09e8289-Abstract.html)). From the same paper (recalled from background knowledge, not re-verified this session), the first component's projection approaches a half-cosine over the trajectory, which is monotonic in elapsed time. If that holds, then pure drift of any origin predicts a one-dimensional mode that correlates strongly with elapsed time. **An $R^2$ of 0.84 with elapsed time therefore cannot by itself distinguish state from artifact.** Shinn showed that PCA produces "phantom oscillations" from smooth, non-oscillatory data, and that **traditional cross-validation does not detect them** ([Shinn 2023, PNAS, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10691246/)). Lebedev and colleagues showed that rotational dynamics can arise from the temporal ordering of single-neuron peaks ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6908571)). Q4 found **no paper that applies DCA, compressed predictive information coding, lagged RRR, CEBRA-time or LFADS at long horizons and reports that the leading component tracks session time**.

On controls, Harris's "Nonsense correlations in neuroscience" is the key reference. He simulated slowly drifting signals and found that methods based on "cross-validation, circular shifting, phase randomization, or detrending gave up to 100% false positive rates." Only pseudo-session tests, session permutation and (if one series is stationary) a linear-shift test were valid ([Harris, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.11.29.402719.full.pdf)). Two parts of that paper matter here. First, a pseudo-session test is valid for *any* scalar statistic, so poor choices can only cost power. Second, it requires that the experimenter randomised the variable, which IBL does for the block sequence. A keyword search of Harris's full text found no discussion of dimensionality reduction or latent variables (same source). The bridge is therefore open. One can define the statistic as the block prior's decodability from a learned predictive subspace, or as the alignment of that subspace with the prior axis. Recomputing it over pseudo-sessions then gives a valid test for an unsupervised method. Elapsed time cannot be tested this way, because it is not randomised. Decoding elapsed time from a subspace is a diagnostic, and calibrating it needs session permutation or covariance-matched random subspaces.

Q4 also found no neural methods paper on normalisation inside cross-validation. With contiguous time folds, which are needed to avoid temporal leakage, a unit that is silent in a training fold has near-zero standard deviation. Z-scoring the test fold with training statistics then divides by almost zero or by a chosen floor, which inflates that unit's test values in a way tied to elapsed time. This is an inference, not a documented result, but it bears directly on a pipeline in which units drift in and out in 14 of 16 sessions.

## Q5: No one has measured where the block prior sits relative to dominant variance

Findling and colleagues treat the prior strictly as a decoding target. They use L1-regularised regression (Lasso), chosen partly "because it is more robust to outliers from single-cell drift", on spike counts from one pre-stimulus window, and they report effect sizes as "corrected $R^2$" (real minus the pseudo-session median). The values are modest: a strong example region, ventrolateral orbitofrontal cortex, reaches 0.16 ([bioRxiv v3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.04.547684v3.full)). The prior that best fits behaviour is an "action kernel", an exponential average of past choices with a median decay of about 5.6 trials. Neural decay constants correlate with the behavioural ones at about 0.3 to 0.4 (same source). Written as an update rule with previous choice $a_t$ and learning rate $\alpha \approx 1/5.6$, the action-kernel prior is

$$\hat{\pi}_{t+1} = (1-\alpha)\,\hat{\pi}_t + \alpha\, a_t .$$

This is a leaky integrator that steps at discrete events, not a continuously evolving autonomous mode. The paper fits no dynamical model to the neural prior signal and calls such a model "a pressing, remaining task" (same source). Two small details matter for replication. The BWM decoding code in this project uses a window of 400 to 100 ms before stimulus onset and 200 pseudo-sessions, while the prior paper's main window is 600 to 100 ms ([local `settings_block.py`, BWM preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.04.547681v2.full.pdf)). The project should state which it used.

The wider literature frames priors and contexts as inputs or initial conditions that shape fast dynamics within a trial, rather than as slow autonomous modes. In monkey medial frontal cortex, context acts as a tonic input that sets the speed of neural trajectories ([Remington et al. 2018, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6009852)), and priors warp the curvature of a low-dimensional manifold ([Sohn et al. 2019, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6805134)). In smooth pursuit, the prior is carried by preparatory activity that sets a gain ([Darlington et al. 2018, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6312195)). Model-based targeted dimensionality reduction recovers task subspaces "despite additional components of neural activity", that is, task axes need not align with dominant variance ([Aoi et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7610668)). On IBL data, Posani and colleagues included the block in their encoding model and found near-maximal "shattering dimensionality" (the fraction of all possible binary splits of task conditions that a linear readout can separate) of about 0.92. They report **no variance share for block or any other variable** ([Posani et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11601379/)). The December 2025 IBL preprint by Liu, Schartner and Fiete reports that the prior modulates integrator and movement populations through "both initial activity bias and gain modulation", without measuring variance share or subspace alignment ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.64898%2F2025.12.15.694430)).

A simple calculation shows why a decodable prior can be absent from the top principal components. Suppose the prior $\pi_t$ adds a small offset of size $\delta$ along a unit direction $u$, on top of noise with covariance $\Sigma$. Its added variance along $u$ is $\delta^2 \operatorname{Var}(\pi)$, and it enters the top-$k$ components only if that quantity is comparable to the $k$-th eigenvalue $\lambda_k$. Linear decodability instead scales with the signal-to-noise quantity

$$\delta^2\, u^\top \Sigma^{-1} u ,$$

which can be large when $u$ points into quiet directions even though the added variance is tiny. The same argument applies to lagged RRR, because it ranks directions by *predicted variance*. A prior offset that is perfectly predictable but small cannot enter a rank-$d$ RRR subspace that is filled by session-scale drift. Absence from the RRR subspace therefore does not show that the prior is non-dynamical. Showing that requires a test of predictability that ignores variance. Options are past-future canonical correlation analysis, DCA, or simply the autocorrelation of the projection onto the prior-decoder axis within blocks and across block switches. Given the movement-and-arousal dominance of top variance in mouse cortex ([Musall et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0502-4); [Stringer et al. 2019, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7029483)), the project's PCA null is expected. It becomes informative only when quantified against a variance-matched random-subspace null.

What remains open, and is not addressed by Findling et al., is the prior's variance share, the orientation of its subspace, whether its axis is autonomously predictable or updated by events, whether it is orthogonal to the slow-drift, arousal and movement dimensions, and whether it is stable across trial epochs. Q5 found **no paper that reports a variance-explained figure for the IBL block prior in any region**.

## Route by route: novelty, scoop risk, framing and the analyses each needs

### Route 1: Is the slow mode a brain state or a recording artifact?

In plain words, this route takes the project's one-dimensional, time-tracking slow mode and asks what produces it, across many brain regions at once. It could be the mouse becoming less aroused, less engaged or sated over the session, or the probe moving relative to the brain so that units fade in and out of the recording.

**Novelty is real for the core decomposition.** No paper found uses recorded probe-motion traces as nuisance regressors for a slow population mode. None applies Cowley-style slow-drift analysis to IBL, Allen or other multi-probe Neuropixels data with motion controls. None tests elapsed time, satiety, engagement and pupil against each other for a brain-wide mouse slow mode (Q1, Q2, Q3). The flagship BWM paper explicitly left this open as "interpretational and technical difficulties" ([peer-review file](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-025-09235-0/MediaObjects/41586_2025_9235_MOESM3_ESM.pdf)).

**Scoop risk is split.** For the pupil, engagement and arousal component it is moderate to high. IBL already has per-trial engagement for all BWM trials and an active pilot on brain-wide stillness and arousal with slow-drift controls, started a month ago ([GitHub engage](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/engage); [GitHub default_mode_network](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/default_mode_network)). Two Hiratani-group projects on IBL internal state are also in progress ([Amjad & Hiratani](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-7749081/v1_covered_14b6566c-ed28-411c-a10a-d12a540353c2.pdf?c=1775216577); [Yin & Hiratani](https://api.biorxiv.org/details/biorxiv/10.1101/2025.07.11.664420)). For the probe-motion-versus-state decomposition and the elapsed-time mode specifically, the risk is low: nothing visible targets them. A preprint from the IBL pilot could plausibly appear in 2027. That timing is a judgement, not something the evidence establishes.

**Recommended framing.** The question should be "What is the dominant slow mode in brain-wide Neuropixels data? A decomposition into probe motion, unit turnover and brain state." The paper should not assert that the mode is an artifact. Kilosort4's finding that large drift is rare in IBL ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732)) means the answer may well be "mostly state". That would still be publishable as a validation, with high-drift sessions serving as positive controls. Engagement should be imported from IBL's public `engage` output and cited, not rebuilt. That turns a competitor's product into a covariate.

**Analyses needed.** The first step is to scale from 16 MOs sessions to the BWM. The paired-recordings table released for the BWM (`brainwidemap/meta/granger.csv`, described in the [peer-review file](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-025-09235-0/MediaObjects/41586_2025_9235_MOESM3_ESM.pdf)) identifies sessions with two simultaneous probes.

The artifact side of the test works insertion by insertion. Load the drift traces and regress each unit's slow rate on the motion at its own depth. Track per-unit amplitude and depth over time from `spikes.amps` and `spikes.depths`, and ask whether the units that drop out first decline in amplitude toward threshold beforehand. Compute presence ratio (the fraction of the session in which a unit fires at all) and amplitude cutoff (the estimated fraction of a unit's spikes lost below the detection threshold). Then replicate the slow mode in sorting-free multi-unit activity per depth bin, which does not depend on unit identities. Because spike-based motion estimates can absorb genuine rate change, add one more independent motion check, either LFP-band motion estimation or a comparison across probes.

The state side of the test asks whether slow modes on two simultaneous probes are shared, judged against a random-walk null in the style of Cowley. It examines the sign structure of the loadings: mixed signs suggest a state, while same-sign, depth-ordered loadings suggest drift. It asks whether loadings depend on firing rate, a state signature, or on spike amplitude, an artifact signature. It computes coherence with pupil, wheel and face motion energy below 0.1 Hz, in the style of Okun. Finally, it compares elapsed time, cumulative reward (a proxy for satiety) and GLM-HMM engagement probability as competing regressors. The sessions in which engagement rises and falls rather than declining steadily are the most valuable here, because they are the only ones that break the collinearity between time and state that Sadeh and Clopath's reviewers flagged.

Running the analysis on the end-of-session passive period is a further option. Whether the mode continues there would separate task engagement from arousal and drift. This suggestion is an inference, not drawn from a cited study.

**Venues.** Precedents for this kind of paper appeared in Neuron ([Cowley et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822647)), Cerebral Cortex ([Okun et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458908)), eLife ([Sadeh & Clopath](https://elifesciences.org/articles/77907v1); [Johnston & Smith](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13246003/)), Current Biology ([Deitch et al.](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(21)01052-6)) and eNeuro ([Johnston et al.](https://www.eneuro.org/content/9/3/ENEURO.0012-22.2022)). A full brain-wide decomposition with a clear answer fits eLife or Current Biology. A narrower version fits eNeuro or Cerebral Cortex.

### Route 2: Long-horizon predictive objectives recover nonstationarity, and here is how to check

In plain words, this route argues that any method that looks for the most predictable or slowest directions in neural activity will, at horizons of tens of seconds, mostly find whatever changes slowly over the session. It then gives researchers a set of tests to tell when a method has found something else.

**Novelty is real as a neuroscience methods contribution but not as a mathematical observation.** The mechanism is classical (Box-Tiao, cointegration, the slowest-process logic of time-lagged independent component analysis, time-contrastive identifiability). The variance-based cousin is already published (Antognini and Sohl-Dickstein; Shinn). What Q4 did not find is any of the following: an empirical demonstration for neural predictive-subspace methods across horizons; a connection to Harris-style valid inference; a pseudo-session test for unsupervised subspaces; or a documented normalisation audit for drifting units. The verified sources support this assessment ([DCA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.09944); [Harris](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.11.29.402719.full.pdf); [Shinn](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10691246/)).

**Scoop risk is low to moderate.** The nearest neighbours are adjacent rather than overlapping. Raju's 2026 arXiv paper on within-session nonstationarity and geometric reliability uses Steinmetz data and representational similarity, not predictive subspaces ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29655)). The GLM-PPSeq poster models slow background rate in IBL motor cortex as a nuisance ([Cosyne 2026](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6102ca347474c263c40150cd/t/69b2ee5acab23a50003b7f26/1773334106922/Cosyne2026_program_book.pdf)). The real threat is a reviewer calling the result obvious, together with a second objection: the slow mode is genuine state, and engagement is itself task-relevant ([Mohammadi et al.](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.1038%2Fs41467-025-66738-0)).

**Recommended framing.** Present the mechanism as an *expected consequence* of the objective, with the time-series lineage cited up front. Put the novelty in three places. The first is empirical: on brain-wide IBL data, the leading predictive subspace rotates from task-aligned to elapsed-time-aligned as the horizon grows, and there is a measurable crossover horizon. The second is inferential: a pseudo-session test for unsupervised subspaces. The third is practical: a control battery. Avoid claiming the mode is artifact. The safe thesis is that long-horizon predictive objectives are dominated by the slowest variance in a session, that in standard datasets this variance is confounded with session time, and that claims that such methods recover task latents need pseudo-session-valid tests.

**Analyses needed.** The work has four parts. The first is simulation. Show that lagged RRR, DCA, past-future canonical correlation and slow feature analysis all recover a time-ordered mode from three kinds of synthetic data: pure drift surrogates matched to the units' autocorrelations, IBL-style injected probe motion, and unit dropout. Use the predictable-fraction formula from the Q4 section to predict the crossover horizon, the lag beyond which drift dominates, and then confirm it empirically. The second part is real data: run the horizon sweeps across many BWM regions, not only MOs.

The third part is inference. Recompute two statistics over pseudo-sessions: how well the block prior can be decoded from each low-rank predictive subspace, and how closely that subspace aligns with the prior axis. Comparing the real values with the pseudo-session distribution gives a valid test ([Harris](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.11.29.402719.full.pdf); [Findling et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408363)).

The fourth part is a set of diagnostics. Decode elapsed time from each subspace dimension, and calibrate that against covariance-matched random subspaces and session permutation. Compute split-half direction reliability against covariance-matched chance, the similarity expected between random directions drawn to match the data's covariance, and report the effective number of independent slow samples. Run trial-locked residualisation in the spirit of communication-subspace and residual-dynamics work. Run high-pass or sliding-mean sensitivity analyses using DCA's own 30-second preprocessing, reported as sensitivity analyses rather than cures, because Harris shows detrending is not guaranteed to work. Audit fold-wise normalisation by reporting the fold structure, the variance floor, the fraction of units affected, and what happens when units with significant rate trends are excluded. Finally, add the probe-motion and unit-stability covariates from Route 1.

Optionally, testing whether a nonlinear contrastive model such as CEBRA-time encodes session time would broaden the audience. Q4 lists this as open.

**Venues.** Methodological precedents appeared in PNAS ([Shinn](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10691246/)), eNeuro ([Garcia et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10897502/); [MEDiCINe](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11896784/)) and NeurIPS ([DCA](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/704cddc91e28d1a5517518b2f12bc321-Abstract.html); [Antognini & Sohl-Dickstein](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/hash/7a576629fef88f3e636afd33b09e8289-Abstract.html)). eNeuro and eLife's reviewed-preprint track fit a neuroscience methods paper. PLOS Computational Biology and the Journal of Neuroscience Methods are natural alternatives, though this is a judgement rather than a sourced precedent. A machine-learning framing with the simulation and inference results in front could target a NeurIPS main-track or workshop paper. A Cosyne abstract is a low-cost way to claim priority.

### Route 3: The block prior lives in neither high-variance nor predictable directions

In plain words, this route asks where in population activity the block prior is carried, across the regions where Findling et al. found it. It tests whether the prior is a small, static offset that shifts each trial's activity, rather than one of the large or slowly evolving patterns that dominate the population.

**Novelty is real but modest.** No paper reports a variance share for the IBL block prior. None tests the prior axis's predictability as distinct from its decodability. None tests its alignment with the slow-drift or arousal axis (Q5, Q3). It genuinely complements Findling et al., who say where the prior is decodable but not how it is organised geometrically ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408363)).

**Scoop risk is low to moderate.** The nearest neighbour is the Liu, Schartner and Fiete preprint on bias and gain ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=10.64898%2F2025.12.15.694430)), and the IBL `prior-localization` repository was still being updated in October 2026 ([IBL GitHub](https://github.com/int-brain-lab)). The bigger risk is that reviewers see the main result as expected, given movement-dominated top variance and Posani's high-dimensional coding.

**Recommended framing.** The claim should be that the prior is carried as a low-variance, event-updated offset, largely orthogonal to the high-variance and autonomously predictable subspace (slow drift, movement, arousal). This connects to the primate literature that treats the prior as an input or initial condition, and it contrasts with a "prior as persistent slow attractor" hypothesis. The paper must not conflate low variance with non-predictable.

**Analyses needed.** On geometry, measure how much of the prior decoder's weight, and how much of its decoding performance, falls inside the leading principal components and the leading RRR modes, against a null of random subspaces matched in variance. Also measure the variance captured along the prior axis and the angle between that axis and the dominant axes. On dynamics, run a predictability test that ignores variance on the projection onto the prior axis: autocorrelation within blocks, past-future canonical correlation, or DCA. Then test whether the projection jumps at action and feedback events rather than drifting smoothly. On mechanism, add a stimulus-by-prior interaction test to detect gain-type effects, which an analysis of baseline offsets would miss. Use pseudo-session nulls throughout. For robustness, report results for both the 600-to-100 ms and the 400-to-100 ms windows, and after residualising pupil and the slow mode.

**Venues.** As a standalone paper this fits eNeuro, the Journal of Neuroscience or PLOS Computational Biology (a judgement rather than a sourced precedent). It is stronger as the application section of Route 2.

## The strongest paper combines the three routes in layers

The recommended path is one paper built in three layers. Route 2 is the spine, because its gaps are best verified, its scoop risk is lowest and it reuses the pipeline already built. The part of Route 1 with low scoop risk is the scientific core: the decomposition into probe motion, unit turnover and elapsed time across two-probe BWM sessions. Engagement and pupil enter as covariates taken from IBL's public outputs, not as headline results. That core answers the reviewer who says "the slow mode is just arousal" or "it is just drift" with data rather than assertion. Route 3 is the worked example of what predictive subspaces miss: the prior is decodable, but it lives in a quiet, event-updated direction that variance-ranked predictive methods cannot see. If the student instead wants a pure neuroscience result, Route 1 in full has the largest payoff. But it means racing or joining IBL staff on the pupil and engagement axis. Given that those staff are publicly building the same covariates, reaching out to the IBL about collaboration is worth considering. This is advice, not a finding.

Several fixes to the existing pipeline should come before any new analysis, because a reviewer will check them first. The normalisation audit belongs at the top of the list. With units drifting in and out in 14 of 16 sessions, z-scoring inside contiguous folds can manufacture part of a time-locked mode. The split-half direction test needs a covariance-matched chance level and an honest count of effective independent samples, since Cowley-style slow drift yields only a handful per session. One research note reports a cluster firing near 141 Hz with a strong late-session drop. If that is in the MOs data, it is a classic candidate for a contaminated or amplitude-drifting multi-unit cluster, and the IBL refractory-period and amplitude-cutoff metrics are the first check ([IBL reproducibility paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12068871)). The prior-decoding window should match one of the IBL conventions, or both should be reported. The project should also check which spike-sorting revision its sessions use and whether drift files exist for each insertion.

Before committing, a short set of checks would settle the remaining novelty uncertainty. The most important is to read the full Amjad and Hiratani and Yin and Hiratani preprints for any within-session neural analysis, and to re-check the `default_mode_network` repository for new reports. Next, crawl the Google Scholar cited-by lists for Cowley 2020, Harris 2020, DCA and Findling 2025, and read the Supplementary Information of both IBL Nature papers. It is also worth extracting the IBL drift-range distribution from Kilosort4's Extended Data Figure 5, which is effectively a census of drift across the BWM. Q3 could not determine whether the 2026 Nature paper by Pachitariu and colleagues on global activity modes, or Bimbard, Harris and Carandini's December 2025 preprint, uses BWM data, so that should be confirmed. Finally, scan the Cosyne 2024 and 2025 and SfN 2024 and 2025 abstracts.

## What this search could not see

The search has real limits, and they bear on the novelty claims above. **No Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar cited-by crawls were possible.** Forward-citation coverage of the anchor papers (Cowley 2020, Harris 2020, DCA, Stringer 2019, Findling 2025) relied on reference lists, on Europe PMC citation-adjacent queries and on targeted searches. A paper citing these anchors without using the search vocabulary could have been missed. **Paywalled and oversized supplements were not read.** These include the Supplementary Information of both IBL Nature papers (only the PMC main text, Methods and Extended Data captions were read), the Nature full text of Steinmetz 2019 and Allen 2019, the Neuropixels 2.0 full text, Kilosort4's Extended Data numbers, the DREDge Nature Methods version, and the IBL spike-sorting white paper on figshare, which returned an access error. Fetches from bioRxiv, PMC and figshare were intermittently rate-limited or blocked, so several preprints were judged from abstracts or snippets.

**Many claims were not re-verified from full text.** In Q1, about half of the 30 included works rest on background knowledge. Q4's claims about CEBRA, LFADS, NDT, POYO, DLAG, communication subspaces, tensor component analysis, tensor maximum-entropy surrogates and the half-cosine shape of random-walk components are all unverified this session, as are Q5's claims about Stringer, Musall, Manley and dPCA variance shares. The Amjad and Hiratani preprint was readable only as a snippet. Its use of BWM neural data, the single most important unresolved overlap for Route 1, is unknown. Grey literature was sampled unevenly. The Cosyne 2026 book was read in full, but Cosyne 2024 and 2025, SfN 2024 and 2025 and FENS abstracts were not. Only the int-brain-lab GitHub organisation was inspected, not the repositories of individual IBL-affiliated labs (Paninski, Pillow, Engel, Fiete, Urai, Churchland), and private IBL working-group channels are invisible. The fMRI decoding literature on drift and autocorrelation was not searched, though it likely holds relevant controls.

Methodologically, this was a rapid systematic review, not a registered one. One agent screened each question, with no dual screening, no formal flow diagram and no risk-of-bias assessment. Counts are approximate, and a general web search engine does not return reproducible result sets. The search date is 17 November 2026, and preprints posted in the last weeks may not yet be indexed. Some figures differ between preprint and published versions (Findling's region counts and the share of sessions in which body movements alone decode the prior), and this report cites the published versions where both were seen.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what the project's central result means. A one-dimensional, time-tracking mode at long horizons is not a surprising discovery about secondary motor cortex. It is what the predictive objective was always going to return in a one-hour session, whatever the mode's cause. That turns a weakness into a contribution. The field has valid tests for supervised decoding under drift, published warnings about PCA under drift, and public probe-motion traces for over 1,500 IBL insertions, but it has never connected them for the unsupervised, predictive methods that are spreading through systems neuroscience. The student's own pipeline already contains every ingredient, including the uncomfortable ones: the drifting units and the irreproducible direction are the evidence the paper needs.

The timing argument favours moving now and choosing ground carefully. IBL staff are publicly building engagement and arousal analyses on the same data, so the defensible territory is the part they have called "technically difficult" and left alone. That territory is the separation of probe motion from state, and the inferential machinery for predictive subspaces. If the slow mode turns out to be mostly genuine state, the paper still stands, and it becomes the first brain-wide mouse counterpart to the macaque slow-drift literature, built on controls that the macaque work never had.
