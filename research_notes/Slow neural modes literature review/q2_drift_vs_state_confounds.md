## Q1. Neuropixels probe motion: magnitude and timescale (acute vs chronic) and the main motion-estimation and sorting tools

### Takeaway
Within-session brain–probe motion in acute head-fixed mouse Neuropixels recordings is typically on the order of tens of µm over hours. Allen reports about 40 µm on average over about 3 h. In the IBL repository, only 11 of more than 500 recordings showed large continuous drift spanning at least 40 µm. Motion is larger in primates and humans. All modern sorters (Kilosort 2.5, 3 and 4, IBL's ibl-sorter, DARTsort) and stand-alone estimators (DREDge, MEDiCINe, and the methods in SpikeInterface) correct drift at second-scale resolution. Benchmarks agree that motion correction remains the dominant residual source of sorting error, and that the estimators can themselves be degraded by nonstationary firing.

### Cited Findings
- **Allen Visual Coding Neuropixels (acute, head-fixed mouse) [V]:** The documentation says "the probe drifts, on average, about 40 microns over the course of the ~3 hour recordings". Units with presence ratio below 0.95 are hidden by default. The docs warn that "all of these metrics assume that the spike waveform is stable throughout the experiment" and recommend plotting amplitude over time to see whether a unit is "drifting below threshold" — [AllenSDK Visual Coding Neuropixels docs](https://allensdk.readthedocs.io/en/latest/visual_coding_neuropixels.html)
- **Kilosort4 (Pachitariu, Sridhar, Pennington, Stringer; Nature Methods 2024; DOI 10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7) [V]:**
  - Drift correction is "directly inherited from previous versions" (Kilosort 2.5). Changes in waveforms over time were attributed mainly to "vertical probe movement or 'drift'".
  - The paper surveyed "a public repository of more than 500 Neuropixel recordings from the IBL consortium" and "found 11 recordings with large, continuous drift that spanned over at least 40 μm, which is the spatial repetition period of a Neuropixels probe".
  - Drift range was defined as the difference between the 5th and 95th percentile of the median drift across channels. The distribution across all IBL recordings is in Extended Data Fig. 5; I did not extract numeric quantiles.
  - The simulated drift types (none, medium, high, fast, step) were "based on patterns of drift identified in the IBL dataset". Hybrid no-drift simulations used IBL recordings "which specifically had very low levels of drift, as estimated by Kilosort2.5".
  - Source: [Europe PMC full text PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732)
- **Neuropixels 2.0 (Steinmetz et al., Science 2021, 372:eabf4588) [S]:**
  - Neuron tracking was more than 90% successful for up to 2 weeks and more than 80% for up to 2 months, and a companion algorithm "largely eliminated the impact of motion" — [UCL / PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8244810)
  - Search summaries of the preprint report a registration-then-Kilosort pipeline, imposed-motion experiments in which correlations between unit firing rate and the motion trace were compared against a shuffle control, the statement that registration does not account for all non-stationarities, and the note that true baseline rates in mouse visual cortex vary over long timescales, so units excluded as unstable may not reflect recording instability — [bioRxiv 2020.10.27.358291](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.10.27.358291v1.full)
  - These preprint claims are [S]: I could not read the full text, and I found no µm magnitude figures.
- **Neuropixels 1.0 (Jun et al., Nature 2017, 551:232–236) [S]:** More than 700 well-isolated units were recorded with two probes, with "good performance out to eight weeks post-implant". I found no drift magnitude in the retrieved snippets — [PubMed 29120427](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29120427/)
- **Chronic mouse implants (Juavinett, Bekheet & Churchland, eLife 2019; DOI 10.7554/eLife.47188) [S]:** The AMIE holder allowed about 100 neurons to be recorded simultaneously in freely moving mice, and the probes could be explanted — [eLife 47188](https://elifesciences.org/articles/47188)
- **Apollo implant (Bimbard et al., eLife 2024) [S]:** Probes were reimplanted up to 6 times with no significant change in quality — [eLife 98522](https://elifesciences.org/articles/98522)
- **Luo et al. 2020 (multi-probe rats):** Not found in my searches; listed under Gaps.
- **Motion is worse in primates and humans (MEDiCINe; Watters, Buccino & Jazayeri; eNeuro 2025, 12(3) ENEURO.0529-24.2025; bioRxiv 2024.11.06.622160) [V]:**
  - The paper states that "Motion is typically more extreme in nonhuman primate (NHP) and human recordings than recordings in rodents."
  - It lists sources of motion: "pulsation, changes in intracranial pressure, decompression of neural tissue after inserting an array, and instability of the mechanical apparatus".
  - MEDiCINe fits a motion function that makes the amplitude-by-depth spike distribution sparse.
  - Its simulations combine linear drift (0.1 µm/s), random walks (SD 1–2 µm/s) and four firing-rate regimes. One regime is periodic firing that is synchronous across neurons with a 4-min period; another has neurons appearing or disappearing with ramping rates. MEDiCINe "outperforms existing motion estimation methods" and improves Kilosort4 accuracy.
  - The paper explicitly notes that "the neural activity itself may be nonstationary: Neuron firing rates may fluctuate over time, neurons may be gained or lost."
  - Source: [Europe PMC PMC11896784](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11896784/)
- **Garcia, Windolf, Boussard, Dichter, Buccino & Yger, eNeuro 2024, 11(2) ENEURO.0229-23.2023 (SpikeInterface motion benchmark; this is the "Garcia 2022/2024" paper, an eNeuro paper rather than eLife) [V]:**
  - Motion correction is broken into three modular steps: spike localization, motion inference, and interpolation. Iterative-template inference is Kilosort's method; decentralized inference follows Boussard 2021.
  - Benchmark scenarios cross 3 drift signals × 2 depth distributions × 2 firing-rate regimes. One regime is homogeneous 5 Hz Poisson firing; the other is sine-modulated firing with a 3-min period, which the reviewers note was meant to test the assumption of stationary rates.
  - Decentralized inference "generally produces lower errors than the iterative template… except for the ZigZag/bimodal/modulated case". The conclusion is that all methods "have some intrinsic limitations that should be taken into account by analysis pipelines."
  - Source: [Europe PMC PMC10897502](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10897502/)
- **SpikeInterface "Opening the black box: a modular approach to spike sorting" (Garcia et al., eLife reviewed preprint 110588, 2025–2026) [S]:** A reviewer noted "the dominant role of motion correction in degrading accuracy" — [eLife RP 110588 reviews](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110588/reviews)
- **DREDge (Windolf, Varol et al., Nature Methods 2025; DOI 10.1038/s41592-025-02614-5; preprint "Robust online multiband drift estimation", bioRxiv 2022.12.04.519043) [S]:**
  - Decentralized registration works from the AP band or the LFP band. The preprint notes that AP-band approaches are limited to about 1 s temporal resolution, so the LFP band allows faster motion tracking.
  - In human intraoperative recordings, DREDge reduced spike-shape variability and sorting error.
  - It is available in SpikeInterface as `dredge_ap` and `dredge_lfp`.
  - Sources: [Columbia Academic Commons](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/phwq-g927/download); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.12.04.519043.full.pdf); [SpikeInterface drift-with-LFP how-to](https://spikeinterface.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/how_to/drift_with_lfp.rst.txt)
- **DARTsort (Boussard, Windolf, Hurwitz, Lee, Yu, Winter, Paninski; bioRxiv 2023.08.11.553023; still a preprint as far as I found) [S]:** A drift-aware registration-and-tracking sorter that "outperforms Kilosort 2.5 on simulated datasets with different drift types". It feeds spike localizations into DREDge. Boussard's thesis models waveform variability as a function of motion instead of interpolating the data — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.08.11.553023.full.pdf); [Kavli listing](https://preprints.kavlimeetings.org/?p=175277)

### Inferences
- In IBL-style acute head-fixed mouse recordings, gross continuous drift of at least 40 µm is uncommon: 11 of more than 500 recordings, per Kilosort4. Typical drift is tens of µm over 1–3 h. Even so, a 20–40 µm shift is comparable to the spatial footprint of a unit's waveform; UnitMatch reports a 10%-amplitude decay distance of 30–95 µm. Modest residual drift can therefore push low-amplitude units below threshold or split and merge them. That would produce the pattern of units effectively silent for a fifth of the session.
- Motion estimators assume that spike density over depth and amplitude is roughly stationary. Garcia 2024 and MEDiCINe both deliberately test slow or synchronous firing-rate modulation, which shows that the tool builders see a two-way confound. Genuine global rate changes could leak into motion estimates, and motion can masquerade as rate change. Garcia's iterative-template (Kilosort) method did comparatively well in one modulated case, so the direction of bias is method-specific.

### Gaps
- I could not verify numeric drift magnitudes from the Neuropixels 2.0 or Jun 2017 full texts: the fetches were blocked and the snippets lacked numbers.
- I did not extract the numeric IBL drift-range distribution from Kilosort4 Extended Data Fig. 5. It would be worth reading directly, because it is effectively a brain-wide drift census of the IBL data.
- Luo et al. 2020 (eLife, chronic multi-probe rats) was not found and is unverified.
- I found no systematic acute-versus-chronic comparison of within-session drift magnitude in a single paper.

## Q2. Has anyone quantified how residual drift, sorting errors or unit turnover bias downstream analyses (dimensionality reduction, latent dynamics, noise correlations, decoding, representational drift)?

### Takeaway
Partially. There is good evidence that sorting errors bias pairwise correlations and single-neuron statistics, plus one simulation preprint showing that sorting distorts population geometry, and evidence that latent trajectories are robust to merging units on the same electrode (Trautmann 2019). Across-session unit turnover is handled by latent alignment (Gallego 2020; Degenhart 2020). I found no study that quantifies how within-session residual drift or unit dropout biases slow latent modes, GPFA or LFADS factors, or the leading principal components. That specific confound, where a slow elapsed-time mode could be produced by units drifting in and out, appears unstudied.

### Cited Findings
- **Laquitaine et al., "Spike sorting biases and information loss in a detailed cortical model," bioRxiv 2024.12.04.626805 (v2; preprint; data on DANDI 001250) [S]:**
  - The study simulates Neuropixels-like recordings in a biophysically detailed rat S1 column of about 30,000 neurons and evaluates modern sorters.
  - Sorters isolated only about 10% of neurons within 50 µm of the shank.
  - Search summaries report that sorting biases tied to firing rate, spike extent, synaptic type and layer cut stimulus discrimination by nearly half, and that sorting distorted manifold geometry while most sorters preserved the overall low-dimensional shape.
  - Whether drift was simulated is unverified.
  - Sources: [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805v2.full); [DANDI 001250](https://dandiarchive.org/dandiset/001250)
- **SLAy (oversplitting errors; PMC12262366, 2025) [S]:** Biased splitting or merging "may compromise the validity of dimensionality reduction and subsequent population-level analyses". The authors acknowledge the counterargument that population methods may be robust to oversplitting, which they did not test — [PMC12262366](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12262366/)
- **Correlations: Pillow, Shlens, Chichilnisky & Simoncelli 2013, and Ventura & Gerkin 2012 ("Accurately estimating neuronal correlation requires a new spike-sorting paradigm", PNAS) [S]:** Sorting spikes independently biases estimates of correlation and synchrony. Sorting mistakes can create artificial correlations in high-rate, synchronous regions — [PubMed 22529350](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22529350/); [Pillow et al. 2013 PDF](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/pub/lcv/pillow12.pdf); see also the review [Stevenson & Kording-style "Building population models… opportunities and pitfalls", arXiv 2102.01807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.01807)
- **Trautmann et al., "Accurate estimation of neural population dynamics without spike sorting," Neuron 2019, 103(2):292–308; DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2019.05.003 [S]:** In monkey motor cortex, including Neuropixels data, pooling threshold crossings per channel "introduces only minor distortions in estimates of neural population state". The justification is random-projection theory. This addresses merge errors, not drift or turnover — [bioRxiv 229252](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/229252v2); [PMC7002296](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7002296)
- **Gallego, Perich, Chowdhury, Solla & Miller, Nature Neuroscience 2020, "Long-term stability of cortical population dynamics underlying consistent behavior" [S]:** Over up to 2 years of Utah-array recordings, latent dynamics were stable "despite steady turnover in the recorded neurons" after per-session linear dimensionality reduction plus alignment. Fixed decoders on raw neurons "degraded substantially". This is across sessions, not within — [bioRxiv 447441](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/447441v3.full.pdf); [PMC7007364](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7007364)
- **Cross-session alignment and BCI literature [S]:** Single-neuron activity drifts and probe movement changes which neurons are recorded, so decoders degrade across days (an Edinburgh unsupervised domain adaptation paper). SPINT (NeurIPS 2025) handles changes in the number and identity of units — [Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/robust-alignment-of-cross-session-recordings-of-neural-population/); [SPINT, NeurIPS 2025](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/24c8d2fe8520746f0084174b75b93ebe-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- **Hierarchical models for nonstationarity (arXiv 1410.3111; pre-2017, foundational) [S]:** This paper notes that nonstationarities "can be caused by fluctuations in anaesthesia level, stability of the physiological preparation or electrode drift". It argues for models that separate these slow effects from faster correlations and dynamics — [arXiv 1410.3111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.3111)
- **Flexible mesh arrays (Nature Biomedical Engineering 2026, "Realignment of representational drift in mouse visual cortex via flexible electrode arrays") [S]:** Latent dynamics recovered after manifold alignment were highly similar across months. Because mesh arrays track the same neurons stably, the drift there is closer to biological drift — [Nature BME](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-026-01780-x)
- **Representational drift versus instability:**
  - **Deitch, Rubin & Ziv, Current Biology 2021 [S]:** Allen Neuropixels plus two-photon data show drift "over timescales spanning minutes to days", driven mostly by changes in activity rate rather than tuning. Their controls include cross-modality agreement with Ca2+ imaging and restricting analyses to units stable across blocks. The authors "cannot completely rule out some contribution" of recording instability — [bioRxiv 2020.10.05.327049](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.10.05.327049v1.full); [Weizmann accepted manuscript](https://weizmann.elsevierpure.com/ws/files/89913110/yz_CurrentBiol_RepresentationalDriftintheMouse_AM_2021.pdf)
  - **Sadeh & Clopath, eLife 2022, 11:e77907 [S]:** Reanalysis of the same Allen Neuropixels data finds that behavioural variability (pupil width, running) contributes significantly to apparent drift. Reviewers objected that the analysis did not control for the passage of time — [eLife 77907](https://elifesciences.org/articles/77907); [peer reviews](https://elifesciences.org/articles/77907/peer-reviews)
  - **Schoonover, Ohashi, Axel & Fink, Nature 2021, 594:541–546 [S]:** Chronic multi-electrode recordings of piriform odor responses over 32 days show drift. The probe was designed to minimize movement, and waveforms were compared against other units. Drift was historically dismissed by some as electrode slippage — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03628-7); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/learning/what-drifting-representations-reveal-about-the-brain)
  - **Marks & Goard and related two-photon work (stimulus-dependent drift in V1; PMC8397766) [S]:** Imaging avoids spike sorting and still finds drift over weeks. It serves as a cross-method control — [PMC8397766](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8397766/)
- **UnitMatch (van Beest, Bimbard, Fabre, …, Carandini; Nature Methods 2024; DOI 10.1038/s41592-024-02440-1) [V]:**
  - Kilosort "correct[s] for electrode drift within a session but naturally cannot correct for drift across sessions that are separately sorted".
  - In chronic recordings, 42 ± 19% of units were matched across consecutive days. In acute recordings, where the probe was reinserted daily, 1.9 ± 3.7% were matched.
  - When sessions were sorted together, 29.5 ± 16.4% of units Kilosort called the same across days were not matched by UnitMatch, showing that sorter identity across time is itself unreliable.
  - Validation used stable "functional fingerprints".
  - Only 25.2 ± 10.2% of sorted units passed quality metrics.
  - Source: [Europe PMC PMC11978519](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11978519/)
- **EMD multi-day tracking (eLife 92495) [S]:** Between-session drift is estimated from firing-rate and amplitude patterns along the probe before matching — [eLife RP 92495](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/92495)
- **Clusterless decoding (arXiv 2512.06280) [S]:** Clusterless models recover information lost to sorting, both from discarded spikes and from reduced model bias — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06280)

### Inferences
- The literature splits along two lines. Across-session turnover is well studied, through alignment, UnitMatch and the representational-drift debate. Within-session nonstationarity from residual drift, sorting instability or unit dropout, and its effect on slow latent modes, is barely studied.
- Neither Trautmann 2019 nor Gallego 2020 tests the scenario at issue here. In that scenario, a subset of units changes presence or gain monotonically with session time. Because those changes are shared in time, PCA, FA or GPFA will put them into a high-variance slow factor that looks like an elapsed-time mode. This is a testable, apparently open gap.
- The representational-drift literature already offers templates for controls: stable-unit restriction, cross-modality replication, regressing out pupil and running, and controlling for elapsed time. These can be ported to within-session slow modes.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that quantifies the effect of residual within-session drift or unit dropout on GPFA, LFADS, PCA or FA latent factors, or on dimensionality estimates. One extended search returned nothing direct.
- Laquitaine et al. figures are secondary. I did not verify whether drift was included or the exact manifold-distortion metrics.
- I found no simulation benchmark that injects realistic IBL-like drift (for example from the Kilosort4 drift simulations) and then measures bias in population latent variables. This would be a natural methods contribution.

## Q3. Methods that explicitly separate state-driven slow firing changes from recording artifacts

### Takeaway
There is no dedicated, validated method for this. Researchers use a toolkit of ad hoc controls: imposed-motion correlation tests, per-unit amplitude, depth and presence-over-time checks, stable-unit restriction, unsorted multi-unit or clusterless signals, motion-trace regressors, arousal covariates (pupil, whisking, EEG), and distributed or simultaneous recordings. The best evidence that slow population drift can be a genuine state signal comes from macaque Utah-array work (Cowley 2020; Johnston 2020) and mouse Neuropixels pupil studies (Okun 2019). Neither used probe-motion regressors.

### Cited Findings
- **Cowley, Snyder, Acar, Williamson, Yu & Smith, "Slow drift of neural activity as a signature of impulsivity in macaque visual and prefrontal cortex," Neuron 2020, 108(3):551–567 [S]:**
  - A shared slow fluctuation over tens of minutes appeared in V4 and was "highly correlated" with a slow drift in PFC recorded on a separate array. It covaried with impulsive behaviour.
  - Distant, simultaneous arrays agreeing is an implicit control against local array artifacts.
  - The follow-up by Johnston et al. (bioRxiv 2020; later Cerebral Cortex / PMC9186107) links slow drift to pupil size, microsaccade rate and EEG alpha, and frames it as a global arousal state.
  - Sources: [PMC7822647](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822647); [CSHL repository](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/41559/); [Johnston et al. PMC9186107](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9186107/)
- **Hennig et al., "Learning is shaped by abrupt changes in neural engagement," Nature Neuroscience 2021, 24:727–736; DOI 10.1038/s41593-021-00822-8 [S]:** Large population fluctuations along a "neural engagement" axis in motor cortex coincide with pupil changes and surprise. They are a state signal identified through behavioural and pupil covariates — [author PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mgolub/publications/2021-Hennig-NN.pdf)
- **Okun et al., "Distinct structure of cortical population activity on fast and infraslow timescales," Cerebral Cortex 2019, 29(5):2196 [S]:** In mouse Neuropixels data, infraslow (≤0.1 Hz) population rate is "highly correlated with arousal as reflected by the pupil area" and is largely set by arousal rather than local factors — [Cerebral Cortex](https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/29/5/2196/5364043); [PMC6458908](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6458908/)
- **"Fast-local and slow-global neural ensembles in the mouse brain" (PMC10312261; authors not verified) [S]:** At slow timescales, ensembles span multiple brain regions. This motivates cross-probe simultaneity as a test, since a probe-local artifact should not be shared across distant probes — [PMC10312261](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10312261/)
- **Brain-wide opposing arousal populations (Nature 2026, "Brainwide blood volume reflects opposing neural populations") [S]:** Functional ultrasound plus Neuropixels found two populations with opposite arousal (whisking) relations across the whole brain. Genuine state signals can therefore have mixed signs across units, unlike a uniform motion-induced loss — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10350-9)
- **Imposed-motion tests (Neuropixels 2.0, Steinmetz 2021) [S]:** Correlation between unit rate and imposed motion is benchmarked against a shuffle from a no-motion epoch. Post-hoc correction increased the yield of units whose rates did not track motion. This is the closest thing to an explicit motion-versus-rate test — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2020/10/28/2020.10.27.358291.full.pdf)
- **Per-unit stability diagnostics:**
  - **Allen [V]:** presence ratio, amplitude cutoff, and amplitude-versus-time inspection — [AllenSDK docs](https://allensdk.readthedocs.io/en/latest/visual_coding_neuropixels.html)
  - **SpikeInterface [S]:** per-unit drift metrics `drift_ptp`, `drift_std` and `drift_mad`, computed from unit depth over time bins — [SpikeInterface drift metrics (0.103.1)](https://spikeinterface.readthedocs.io/en/0.103.1/modules/qualitymetrics/drift.html)
  - **UnitMatch [V]:** reports per-unit "amount of drift", presence ratio and peak amplitude among its quality features — [PMC11978519](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11978519/)
- **IBL's own artifact logic (IBL reproducibility paper; International Brain Laboratory et al., eLife 2025, 100840; PMC12068871) [V]:**
  - "severe discontinuity will cause units to artifactually disappear/reappear".
  - Drift QC overlays the drift estimate on the raster "to detect sharp displacements as a function of time". Quantitatively, "drift is the cumulative absolute electrode motion estimated during spike sorting (μm)".
  - Source: [Europe PMC PMC12068871](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12068871)
- **Sorting-free population signals:** Trautmann 2019 used multi-unit threshold crossings [S], and clusterless decoding is another option [S]. Both bypass unit identity, so a slow mode that survives in sorting-free signals is less likely to reflect cluster turnover — [Trautmann bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/229252v2); [arXiv 2512.06280](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06280)
- **Spike detection under amplitude drift [S]:** Fixed thresholds make units near threshold lose spikes or disappear as amplitude drifts. Normalized template matching recovers them. Adaptive thresholds keep detection rates stable as amplitude decays over 200 days. These come from implant and BCI engineering, not population analysis — [Sci Rep 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-48456-y); [J Neural Eng 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1741-2552/accece)
- **Brain-state effects concentrate in slow-firing neurons (Cell Reports 2023, "Brain state transitions primarily impact the spontaneous rate of slow-firing neurons") [S]:** Genuine state effects have a characteristic signature: changes in the slow-firing population rather than amplitude-dependent loss — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S221112472301197X)

### Inferences
- A defensible separation strategy can be assembled from existing pieces, but nobody appears to have combined them. The pieces, with the signature each predicts:
  1. **Probe-motion regressors** (IBL `drift.um` per depth block). An artifact would make units change with local motion at their depth.
  2. **Per-unit amplitude and depth trajectories.** Artifact-driven dropout should be preceded by amplitude decline toward threshold, or by a shift in depth. A genuine rate change should leave amplitude unchanged.
  3. **Two-probe simultaneity.** A genuine global state should be shared across probes and regions with mixed signs. Probe-local motion artifacts should not be shared, or should follow each probe's own motion trace.
  4. **Pupil, wheel and lick covariates, plus task engagement.** Following Okun 2019, Cowley 2020 and Sadeh & Clopath 2022, a genuine state mode should be predicted by these covariates.
  5. **Sorting-free replication** using multi-unit or threshold-crossing rates per depth bin. A genuine mode should survive without unit identities.
- There is a subtle two-way confound. Motion estimators are built from spike-amplitude and depth histograms, and Garcia 2024 and MEDiCINe show their accuracy depends on firing-rate nonstationarity. So a strong, depth-structured, genuine rate change could in principle bias the drift estimate. Using the IBL drift trace as a regressor is therefore not perfectly independent of neural state. LFP-band motion estimation (DREDge LFP) or cross-probe comparisons would add independence.
- A single cluster firing at 141 Hz with a strong late-session rate drop is a classic candidate for a multi-unit or contaminated cluster, or one losing spikes to amplitude drift. The IBL refractory-period and amplitude-cutoff metrics are the first check.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that uses recorded probe-motion traces as nuisance regressors in a latent-variable model of slow population activity, or that jointly models motion and neural state.
- I found no paper that applies Cowley-style slow-drift analysis to IBL, Allen, or other multi-probe Neuropixels data with motion controls.
- The Neuropixels 2.0 imposed-motion analysis details (effect sizes, thresholds) are [S]. I could not read the full text.

## Q4. Does IBL publish per-insertion probe motion (drift) estimates, and how were they computed?

### Takeaway
Yes, verified directly from the public IBL database. `drift.um.npy`, `drift.times.npy` and `drift_depths.um.npy` exist for 1,527 probe-insertion collections on OpenAlyx, under `alf/probeXX/pykilosort`, with some re-sorted at revision `#2024-05-06#`. They are the non-rigid, block-wise motion estimates from the Kilosort-2.5-derived ibl-sorter, on roughly 2.19 s time bins with 9 depth blocks spaced 400 µm apart. Method documentation lives in the IBL spike-sorting white paper (figshare DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.19705522, v3/v4), which I could not open.

- **A separate, simpler IBL drift estimator [V]:** `brainbox.metrics.electrode_drift.estimate_drift(spike_times, spike_amps, spike_depths)` builds a time × depth × amplitude histogram. Its settings are 1 s bins, 2 µm depth bins and one amplitude bin. It cross-correlates each time slice with the median depth profile within ±50 depth samples (about ±100 µm), uses a parabolic peak, applies 9-sample Hanning smoothing, and subtracts the mean. It returns a single (rigid) drift trace per second. It can recompute a rigid drift trace from released spikes if the sorter's drift files are missing — [IBL docs source](https://docs.internationalbrainlab.org/_modules/brainbox/metrics/electrode_drift.html)

In the repeated-site dataset, "1 recording fail[ed] due to drift" on manual inspection, compared with 10 for noisy channels. ### Inferences
- The proposed brain-wide test is feasible with public data. Per-insertion, depth-resolved motion traces exist on about 2.2 s bins, finer than the slow modes of interest. Many BWM sessions have two simultaneous probes, so motion and neural state can be compared within and across probes.
- Because the drift files come from the same spike data used for sorting, and are estimated before registration, they record the motion that was corrected. They do not capture residual uncorrected motion or sorting instability. Residual artifacts would need per-unit amplitude and depth-over-time diagnostics as well. IBL `spikes.depths` and `spikes.amps` allow these per spike.

## Q5. Overall assessment: has the proposed brain-wide "slow mode: drift or state?" test been done, and what remains open?

### Takeaway
I found no published or preprinted study that tests, brain-wide or in IBL data, whether dominant slow or elapsed-time population modes are explained by probe motion and sorting instability versus brain state, using per-insertion motion traces, two-probe simultaneity, pupil and behaviour. Adjacent work exists on each component: slow drift as arousal in macaque, infraslow population rate tied to pupil in mouse, behaviour versus representational drift, drift-correction benchmarks, and sorting-bias simulations. The combination appears open.

### Cited Findings
- Genuine slow population drift linked to arousal and impulsivity is established in macaque (Cowley 2020; Johnston 2020) and mouse (Okun 2019). None of these used probe-motion regressors — [PMC7822647](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822647); [PMC9186107](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9186107/); [PMC6458908](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6458908/)
- Behaviour can masquerade as drift, and the reviewers of that claim asked for elapsed-time controls — [eLife 77907](https://elifesciences.org/articles/77907/peer-reviews)
- Motion-correction benchmarks acknowledge residual errors and firing-rate nonstationarity, but evaluate sorting accuracy, not downstream latent structure — [Garcia 2024 eNeuro](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10897502/); [MEDiCINe eNeuro 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11896784/)
- IBL treats drift only as a recording-level QC gate, and notes that drift discontinuities make units "artifactually disappear/reappear" — [PMC12068871](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12068871)
- My extended searches for slow drift together with elapsed time or session time in brain-wide mouse data found arousal-linked work but nothing on elapsed-time modes controlled for probe motion — [search-surfaced examples: Nature 2026 arousal populations](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10350-9); [bioRxiv sleep-state ensembles 2026](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.13.744563v1.full)

### Inferences
- **Novelty:** The proposed project fills a real gap. It would quantify, across about 100+ BWM regions, how much of the leading slow population mode is explained by recorded motion at each unit's depth, per-unit amplitude decline, presence ratio and turnover, and pupil, engagement and lapse covariates. Two-probe sessions provide a natural test: shared versus probe-specific slow modes.
- **Secondary methods contribution:** Inject IBL-like drift (Kilosort4 drift simulations or SpikeInterface/MEArec generators) into hybrid recordings with known latent dynamics, then measure bias in PCA, FA and GPFA slow factors. Nothing like this appeared in my searches.
- **Risk:** IBL head-fixed drift is usually small (11 of more than 500 with large drift per Kilosort4). The result may be that most slow modes are genuine state, which is still publishable as a validation. Sessions with large drift offer natural positive controls.

### Gaps
- My searches may have missed very recent (late-2026) preprints and conference abstracts (SfN, Cosyne). Before committing, search bioRxiv directly for "IBL drift" plus "latent" and "slow drift" plus "Neuropixels" sorted by date, and check the IBL Brain-Wide Map companion papers' supplements for any elapsed-time or motion control.
- I did not check whether IBL BWM papers (e.g., the Nature 2025 BWM paper or "Brain-wide representations of prior information") included session-time or drift covariates in their encoding or decoding models.
